RE: Statement in Support of Claim — John Q. Sample, Lower Back Condition

My name is Mike Doe. I worked alongside John Sample at the Riverside Warehouse from 2012 until he left in December 2018, and I saw him at work five days a week for nearly all of that time. We have stayed close since he left: we talk on the phone most weeks and see each other in person about once a month. Everything below is either what I personally saw or what John told me, and I have marked which is which.

September 2014 — the day it started.

We were loading pallets onto a delivery truck on a night shift. John bent down to muscle a pallet of engine parts — 80 pounds if it was an ounce — and I heard him cry out from two rows over. I have known John since high school and I had never heard him make a sound like that. He grabbed his lower back with both hands and could not straighten up. He stayed frozen a moment, then sat down hard on a crate and stayed there about twenty minutes while the rest of us worked around him. I took the last few pallets myself because he physically could not lift. Our supervisor saw it, shook his head, and sent him home before the shift ended. To my knowledge no incident report was ever filed — I remember being surprised, because I figured an injury like that would mean paperwork. For days afterward he walked stiff and sideways, one hand pressed against his lower back.

2014 to 2016 — the belt, the light duty, the break-room floor.

John was never the same after that shift. Some days he wore a stiff back belt under his shirt — you could see the outline when he reached up. By 2015 he was off the heavy loads for good, moved to the scanning station, and honestly the shift still never let up on him. I lost count of the times he bent for a dropped clipboard or a scanner cord and just stopped halfway — hand on his knee, stuck there for a few seconds before he could finish the motion. Anything off the floor, he picked up slow, sideways, one hand braced on the rack. Long shifts wrecked him. In 2016 there were stretches where I'd come back from break and find John flat on his back on the break-room floor, knees bent, staring at the ceiling, waiting out the pain until he could stand up — sometimes close to an hour. He never wanted a fuss made about it.

2017 — the week he lost, and the shots.

In the summer of 2017 he moved one stack of pallets wrong and missed almost the entire week of work. That was when he first told me the pain had started running out of his back and down his left leg into his foot — he said it felt like someone was standing on the leg. After that he started favoring that leg when he walked, and I would see him wince and grab the door frame getting out of the forklift. That fall he told me the doctor was giving him shots deep in his back. The first round helped — he was nearly himself for a few months, and I remember thinking he had beaten the thing.

2018 — the year the job ground him down.

Through 2018 I watched the job take him apart piece by piece. He stopped asking to be on the heavy crew. He would wait — sometimes fifteen minutes — for a younger guy to free up rather than lift something on his own. He got written up once for slow picks. John, who used to be the fastest loader in the building. He took the write-up without a word, because arguing about it meant explaining the back. By fall he was down to four-hour days, and even on those he took breaks he never clocked — lying in the break room or sitting in his car with the seat reclined. I asked him once why he didn't take the forklift spot full-time, and he said sitting that long hurt too much to run a shift. In December 2018 he left the warehouse. He told me later that he never really decided to quit — he just finally admitted to himself he couldn't do the work anymore, and he was tired of being the reason the line fell behind. I remember his last day. He cleaned out his locker fast and didn't say much, and that was not like John.

Since he left — what I still see.

We stayed close after he left. The fishing trips stopped first — he used to be the one who organized them. At the company picnic in 2022 he tried to help set up tables and had to quit after ten minutes; he sat on the ground with his legs stuck straight out in front of him and had to roll onto his side and push off a park bench to get up. When I visit the house now, he is usually shifting his weight foot to foot partway through the visit, or lying flat on the living-room floor in the middle of a conversation. He has told me his wife has to help him with his socks on bad mornings, and that he wakes up two or three nights a week from the pain. He has also told me he has had two more rounds of those back shots since leaving, and that the recent ones have not helped much.

What the work cost him.

John went from the fastest loader in the building to a man who could not do the job at all, in about four years, and I watched every step of it. He works part-time now — answering phones for a delivery company, a desk job, about 24 hours a week — because nothing physical is possible for him anymore. He has told me he makes roughly a third less than he did at the warehouse and turns down the extra shifts he cannot sit through. He never wanted to leave that warehouse. He liked the work and he was proud of being good at it. The back took it from him slowly, shift by shift, and watching that happen is the most honest thing I can tell you about what this condition has done to him.
