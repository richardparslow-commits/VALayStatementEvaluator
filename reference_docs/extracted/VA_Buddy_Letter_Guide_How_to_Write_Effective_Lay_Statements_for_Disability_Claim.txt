A VA “buddy letter” (also called a lay statement) is a written account
from someone who has direct knowledge of a veteranʼs condition or
service experience. These statements can play an important role in VA
disability claims by describing the veteranʼs symptoms, daily limitations,
or in-service events that may not be fully captured in medical or service
records.
Authority:Under 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b) and 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.159(a)(2),
3.303(a), lay evidence is considered competent if it relates to matters
the person observed and is relevant to the claim.
Why Buddy Letters Matter
Buddy letters are supporting evidence, not determinative proof. The VA
weighs them along with medical and service records. They can help:
Corroborate an in-service stressor or injury (especially when
official records are incomplete).
Document observable symptoms and their effect on work, family,
or social functioning.
Provide evidence of continuity of symptoms after service.
⚠ Correction to the original article:The claim that “80% of veterans
receive initial ratings that are too low” is not supported by VA data or
authoritative studies. While veterans often disagree with initial ratings,
no statutory or regulatory authority supports a specific percentage.

Building a Compelling Narrative
Effective buddy letters translate a veteranʼs experience into plain,
observable facts. The VA needs specifics:
Dates or time frames.
Clear examples of behavior or limitations.
Observable changes since service.
Example:Instead of “He has trouble sleeping,” write:“Since his 2020
deployment, he wakes multiple times at night from nightmares, often
pacing the house for an hour before returning to bed.”
Strategic Use in Claims
Buddy letters can be submitted:
With an initial claimto help establish service connection.
With a request for increased ratingto demonstrate worsening
symptoms.

Authority:See 38 C.F.R. §§ 3.156, 3.159; VA adjudicators must consider
all lay and medical evidence of record.
⚠ Correction to the original article:While timing can help (for
example, submitting early ensures the VA considers the statement with
the initial evidence), holding letters “in reserve” is a strategy sometimes
used by advocates but not something recognized in statute or
regulation. Veterans may submit new and material evidence at any stage
(38 C.F.R. § 3.156).
Crafting Effective Buddy Letters
Strong buddy letters share these traits:
Credibility:Avoid exaggerations or overly emotional claims. Stick
to facts observed.
Relevance:Connect the observations to the specific disability
being claimed.
Clarity:Use short paragraphs or bullet points for key examples.
Key Areas to Document
Behavioral changes(mood swings, irritability, social withdrawal).
Physical limitations(difficulty walking, lifting, or performing daily
activities).
Cognitive or mental health effects(memory problems,
concentration issues).

⚠ Important:Lay statements cannot replace medical diagnoses. They
can support what a doctor has said, but they do not establish medical
causation (seeJandreau v. Nicholson, 492 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007)).
Formatting and Organization
Include:
Your name, relationship to the veteran, and contact information.
A clear statement of purpose (supporting the veteranʼs claim for
[condition]).
Specific observations, organized by date or theme.
A closing statement reaffirming your support and truthfulness (sign
and date).
Sample Opening:
“I am writing to support [Veteranʼs Name] in his claim for VA disability
benefits for [condition]. I have known him since [year] as his
[spouse/friend/fellow service member]. I have personally observed the
following…”
Avoiding Common Mistakes
Donʼt make medical diagnoses (leave that to doctors).
Donʼt rely on vague statements (“Heʼs always been different”).
Donʼt conflict with established evidence—be consistent with
service and medical records.
Avoid overly emotional or accusatory language.

When combined with service records, medical opinions, and other
documentation, buddy letters can help establish service connection or
justify a higher rating. The VA must consider them as part of the “benefit
of the doubt” standard under 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b).
Key Takeaways
Buddy letters arecompetent lay evidencewhen they describe
what the writer has observed.
They cannot diagnose conditions but can document theeffectsof
a condition.
They should be specific, credible, and tied to the claimed disability.
They are most effective when paired with medical and service
documentation.
