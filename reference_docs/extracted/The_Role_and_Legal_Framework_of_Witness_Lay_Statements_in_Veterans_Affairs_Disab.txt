The Role and Legal Framework of 
Witness Lay Statements in Veterans 
Affairs Disability Adjudication 
Introduction to the Veterans Affairs Adjudicatory 
Framework 
The United States Department of Veterans Affairs (VA) operates one of the most 
extensive and complex administrative adjudicatory systems globally, responsible for 
processing millions of disability compensation claims annually. Unlike traditional civil 
litigation, which is structurally adversarial, the VA disability claims process is statutorily 
mandated to be ex parte, paternalistic, and non-adversarial in nature. Within this 
uniquely claimant-friendly framework, the traditional burden of proof is significantly 
modified. The VA bears an affirmative, statutory "duty to assist" veterans in developing 
the evidentiary record necessary to substantiate their claims, ensuring that those who 
have borne the battle are not denied rightful compensation due to procedural 
complexities or historical documentation failures. 
To successfully establish direct service connection for a physical or psychiatric 
disability, a claimant must satisfy a rigorous three-element legal test articulated by the 
United States Court of Appeals for Veterans Claims (CAVC) in Caluza v. Brown. The 
Caluza framework requires: (1) competent medical evidence of a current, diagnosed 
disability; (2) medical or, in certain circumstances, lay evidence of an in-service 
incurrence or aggravation of a disease or injury; and (3) medical evidence establishing a 
nexus, or causal relationship, between the current disability and the in-service event. 
While clinical records and expert medical opinions serve as the primary instruments 
used to satisfy the first and third elements, the second element—the in-service event—
frequently relies on the lived experiences and observations of the veteran and their 
peers. 
This is where witness lay statements, colloquially referred to in the veteran community 
as "buddy statements," become critical to the adjudicative process. Lay evidence is 
formally defined as any testimony or written statement provided by an individual who 
lacks specialized education, training, or experience in a medical or technical field, but 
who possesses firsthand knowledge of facts and circumstances that can be readily 
observed and described by an ordinary person. Because official military service records 
are often incomplete, lost to historical tragedies, or silent regarding specific localized 
injuries or gradual behavioral changes, lay statements serve as the indispensable 
evidentiary bridge that connects a veteran's current medical reality to their historical 
military service. This comprehensive report provides an exhaustive analysis of the 
strategic purpose, statutory foundations, regulatory frameworks, defining jurisprudence, 
and procedural mechanics governing the use of lay statements in VA disability claims. 
The Strategic Purpose and Utility of Lay Evidence 
The fundamental purpose of a lay statement within the VA claims ecosystem is to inject 
firsthand, observable facts into an administrative record that might otherwise be 
dominated by sterile, generalized, and occasionally incomplete medical data. Lay 

----- Page 2 -----
evidence can support virtually any component of a VA disability claim, but its utility is 
most pronounced in several specific evidentiary scenarios: filling historical 
documentation gaps, establishing a continuous chain of symptomatology, 
demonstrating the real-world severity and functional impact of a disability, and 
countering inadequate clinical examinations. 
Remediating Gaps in Military and Medical Records 
Military service, particularly in combat, austere environments, or specialized training 
scenarios, is inherently non-conducive to meticulous medical record-keeping. Service 
Treatment Records (STRs) frequently omit minor orthopedic injuries, field treatments 
provided by combat medics, or early psychiatric symptoms that service members may 
have deliberately avoided reporting due to institutional stigma or operational tempo. 
Furthermore, catastrophic historical events—most notably the 1973 National Personnel 
Records Center (NPRC) fire in St. Louis—destroyed millions of official military files, 
leaving generations of veterans without formal, contemporaneous documentation of 
their service operations or injuries. 
In the total or partial absence of STRs, a lay statement from a fellow service member 
can independently establish the occurrence of an in-service event. An effective lay 
statement operates by describing concrete, observable facts: the approximate date and 
geographic location of an incident, the mechanical nature of a training accident, a visible 
physical injury, or the immediate behavioral aftermath of a traumatic event. By providing 
these highly specific, contextual details, a lay witness transforms a generalized, 
uncorroborated claim into a verifiable historical fact that the VA is legally obligated to 
weigh and consider in its adjudicative process. 
Corroborating Stressors via the Joint Services Records Research Center 
When a veteran claims Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD) based on a non-combat 
stressor, the VA generally requires corroboration that the stressful event occurred. If the 
veteran's STRs do not document the event, the VA utilizes the Joint Services Records 
Research Center (JSRRC), a Department of Defense facility that researches historical 
unit records, after-action reports, and ship logs to verify the occurrence of the claimed 
stressor. 
Lay statements are critical in this process. A veteran's own detailed lay statement, 
supported by buddy statements from unit peers, provides the specific parameters—
exact dates, unit designations, geographic coordinates, and incident descriptions—that 
the JSRRC requires to conduct a successful archival search. Without the factual 
foundation laid by comprehensive lay evidence, the JSRRC cannot effectively 
corroborate the stressor, often resulting in the denial of the PTSD claim. 
Establishing Continuity of Symptomatology 
Under 38 C.F.R. § 3.303(b), veterans can establish service connection for chronic 
conditions by demonstrating a "continuity of symptomatology" from their time in active 
service through the present day. This regulatory pathway is vital for veterans whose 
conditions were briefly noted in service but were not formally diagnosed as chronic 
pathological conditions until years or decades after their discharge. 
Contemporaneous medical evidence alone is frequently insufficient to prove this 
continuity because individuals rarely seek uninterrupted, documented medical treatment 
for chronic, manageable pain or symptoms over a span of several decades. In this 
context, lay testimony from family members, spouses, or lifelong friends becomes an 

----- Page 3 -----
indispensable evidentiary tool. As affirmed in cases like Savage v. Gober and Wilson v. 
Derwinski, symptoms, rather than formal clinical treatment, are the essence of continuity 
of symptomatology. Witnesses can attest to the uninterrupted presence of observable 
symptoms—such as a veteran consistently walking with an altered gait, complaining of 
lower back pain after moderate exertion, or utilizing over-the-counter braces and 
medications—effectively bridging the temporal gap between military discharge and a 
current, formal medical diagnosis. 
Demonstrating Disability Severity and Occupational Impairment 
While a medical provider can formally diagnose a condition and outline its clinical and 
physiological parameters, a physician during a brief clinical encounter rarely observes 
how that condition functionally affects a veteran's daily life, relationships, or 
employability. Lay statements from spouses, adult children, or employers provide vital, 
qualitative context regarding the true severity of a disability. 
For example, in psychiatric claims such as Major Depressive Disorder or PTSD, a 
spouse is uniquely positioned to document the frequency of night terrors, the severity of 
panic attacks, the presence of suicidal ideation, or the extent of social isolation. In 
orthopedic or neurological claims, an employer or coworker can attest to a veteran's 
inability to perform specific physical tasks, lift heavy objects, or maintain regular 
attendance due to chronic pain or fatigue. This qualitative lay evidence is heavily 
weighed by Rating Veterans Service Representatives (RVSRs) when assigning a 
specific disability percentage, as the VA rating schedule is fundamentally predicated on 
compensating for the average impairment of earning capacity caused by the disability. 
Countering Inadequate Compensation and Pension (C&P) Examinations 
The VA relies overwhelmingly on contracted medical examiners to conduct 
Compensation and Pension (C&P) examinations, which form the medical basis for most 
rating decisions. However, these contracted examiners are often constrained by strict 
time limits and may fail to capture the full, longitudinal scope of a veteran's condition, 
particularly if the veteran happens to be experiencing a symptomatically "good day" 
during the brief examination window. 
Detailed lay statements submitted prior to the C&P examination create an unalterable 
factual baseline in the administrative record. If an examiner issues an unfavorable or 
dismissive medical opinion that minimizes the severity of a condition while 
simultaneously failing to address the conflicting, detailed lay evidence already in the file, 
the veteran can utilize administrative and legal mechanisms to challenge the adequacy 
of the examination. An examiner's failure to engage with credible lay evidence renders 
the resulting medical opinion legally insufficient, allowing the veteran to demand a new, 
adequate examination. 
Statutory Foundations of Lay Evidence 
The integration of lay evidence into the VA adjudicatory process is not merely an 
administrative policy preference; it is a rigid statutory mandate codified by the United 
States Congress. Several key provisions of Title 38 of the United States Code establish 
the bedrock principles governing how the VA must receive, evaluate, and weigh non-
expert testimony.

38 U.S.C. § 5107: Benefit of the Doubt and Equipoise 
The cornerstone of the non-adversarial VA system is the "Benefit of the Doubt" doctrine, 
formally codified at 38 U.S.C. § 5107(b). The statute explicitly mandates that the 
Secretary of Veterans Affairs "shall consider all information and lay and medical 
evidence of record in a case". Furthermore, when there is an "approximate balance of 
positive and negative evidence regarding any issue material to the determination of a 
matter," the Secretary is legally required to give the benefit of the doubt to the claimant. 
This standard, often referred to as "equipoise," represents a significant departure from 
the standard of proof utilized in civil litigation. In traditional courts, a plaintiff must prove 
their case by a preponderance of the evidence; in the VA system, if the evidence 
weighing against a claim is merely equal to the evidence supporting it, the tie goes to 
the veteran. This statutory directive elevates the strategic importance of lay evidence. A 
highly credible, detailed lay statement from a spouse or peer can provide the precise 
evidentiary weight necessary to shift a claim from a state of negative evidence into a 
state of equipoise, thereby triggering the benefit of the doubt rule and securing a grant 
of benefits. 
38 U.S.C. § 1154: Due Consideration and the Combat Veteran Exception 

----- Page 5 -----
Congress recognized that the chaotic realities of military service—particularly in active 
combat zones—often preclude the generation of traditional, methodical medical 
evidence. To address this, 38 U.S.C. § 1154(a) requires the VA to give "due 
consideration" to the "places, types, and circumstances of such veteran’s service" when 
evaluating both medical and lay evidence. 
More significantly, 38 U.S.C. § 1154(b) establishes a powerful, highly protective 
evidentiary exception specifically for combat veterans. If a veteran engaged in combat 
with the enemy, the VA must accept "satisfactory lay or other evidence" that an injury or 
disease was incurred or aggravated in service, provided the evidence is consistent with 
the general circumstances, hardships, and conditions of that combat service. This 
statutory provision explicitly allows a combat veteran's lay statement alone to establish 
the in-service event element of the Caluza test, even if absolutely no official medical or 
personnel record exists documenting the injury. 
To overcome this powerful statutory presumption, the government cannot rely on mere 
speculation or the simple absence of contemporaneous medical records; the VA must 
produce "clear and convincing evidence to the contrary" to successfully rebut the 
veteran's lay testimony. This elevated burden of rebuttal—requiring a standard much 
higher than a mere preponderance of evidence—is one of the strongest statutory 
protections afforded to combat veterans within the broader administrative law 
framework. 
38 U.S.C. § 5103A: The Statutory Duty to Assist 
Under the Veterans Claims Assistance Act (VCAA), codified at 38 U.S.C. § 5103A, the 
VA has a statutory duty to make reasonable efforts to assist claimants in obtaining the 
evidence necessary to substantiate their claims. This duty includes a specific obligation 
to provide a VA medical examination or obtain an independent medical opinion if the 
record contains sufficient competent lay or medical evidence to indicate that a current 
disability may be associated with military service. Lay statements are the primary, most 
accessible mechanism by which veterans trigger this statutory duty to assist, legally 
forcing the VA to provide a clinical evaluation and nexus opinion even when historical 
military medical records are entirely silent. 
Regulatory Framework and Specific Directives 
To operationalize the broad mandates of Title 38 of the United States Code, the VA has 
promulgated extensive regulations within Title 38 of the Code of Federal Regulations 
(C.F.R.). These regulations dictate exactly how adjudicators must weigh lay testimony in 
specific clinical scenarios. 
Defining Competent Lay Evidence: 38 C.F.R. § 3.159 
The defining parameters of lay evidence are established in 38 C.F.R. § 3.159(a)(2). The 
regulation states that "competent lay evidence" is any evidence that does not require 
the proponent to have specialized education, training, or experience. Evidence is 
considered legally competent if it is provided by an individual who has personal 
knowledge of facts or circumstances and conveys matters that can be physically 
observed and described by an ordinary layperson. 
This regulatory definition draws a strict, critical line between factual observation and 
expert clinical conclusion. A layperson is fully competent to state, "I witnessed the 
veteran fall from a transport vehicle and clutch his knee in visible agony," but a 
layperson is generally not competent to state, "The veteran suffered a torn anterior 

----- Page 6 -----
cruciate ligament and meniscus due to the fall". The former is a competent, observable 
fact; the latter is a medical diagnosis requiring clinical expertise, which generally falls 
outside the scope of lay competence unless the layperson is merely reporting what a 
medical professional told them at the time. 
specialized Rules for PTSD and Military Sexual Trauma: 38 C.F.R. § 3.304 
While 38 C.F.R. § 3.303 governs the general principles of service connection, 38 C.F.R. 
§ 3.304 outlines highly specific evidentiary rules for establishing service connection for 
PTSD. Recognizing the psychological complexities of trauma, the regulation provides 
distinct evidentiary pathways based on the nature of the stressor: 
1. Combat Stressors: Consistent with 1154(b), if the veteran engaged in combat, 
lay testimony alone establishes the stressor absent clear and convincing 
evidence to the contrary. 
2. Fear of Hostile Military or Terrorist Activity: If a non-combat stressor relates 
to a fear of hostile activity (e.g., incoming mortar fire, improvised explosive 
devices), a VA psychiatrist or psychologist can formally confirm that the claimed 
stressor is adequate to support a PTSD diagnosis. In doing so, the clinician relies 
heavily on the veteran's lay account of the circumstances, which serves as the 
foundational evidence. 
3. Former Prisoners of War (POWs): Under § 3.304(e), for former POWs, the 
omission of clinical records made upon repatriation is not determinative, and lay 
evidence from comrades regarding disabilities incurred during confinement is 
granted significant weight. 
4. Military Sexual Trauma (MST): 38 C.F.R. § 3.304(f)(5) acknowledges the 
systemic reality that MST is rarely reported through official military or law 
enforcement channels at the time of the incident. Consequently, the regulation 
explicitly permits the use of indirect lay evidence, known as "markers," to 
corroborate the veteran's account. These markers include lay evidence of 
behavioral changes such as sudden requests for transfer, a sharp deterioration in 
previously stellar work performance, the onset of substance abuse, unexplained 
episodes of depression or panic attacks, or statements from roommates, family 
members, or clergy who noticed a distinct change in the veteran's demeanor. A 
qualified clinician's interpretation of these behavioral lay markers can serve as 
formal legal corroboration that the trauma occurred, effectively bridging the 
evidentiary gap created by the institutional silence surrounding sexual violence. 
Internal Agency Adjudication: The M21-1 Procedures 
Manual 
While the C.F.R. provides the regulatory law, the M21-1 Adjudication Procedures 
Manual serves as the VA's internal administrative guidebook. It provides binding, step-
by-step procedural instructions for RVSRs and claims processors on how to handle 
evidence on a daily basis. 
Part V, Subpart ii, Chapter 1, Section B of the M21-1 manual specifically governs the 
review of testimony and lay evidence. The manual instructs adjudicators that lay 
evidence must be afforded probative value if the reporter has direct personal 
knowledge, accurate memory, and the competence to report the observed facts. 

----- Page 7 -----
Crucially, M21-1 section V.ii.1.B.2.c explicitly addresses the description of symptoms as 
evidence. The manual directs that a claimant's own statement covering a condition 
within their ability to describe (e.g., their own physical pain, limited range of motion, 
subjective mental distress, or progression of symptoms over time) constitutes valid, 
actionable evidence. The manual acknowledges that while such statements inevitably 
possess self-serving aspects, the claimant is functionally the most qualified source to 
describe the specific circumstances and disabling effects of their own disease or injury. 
Furthermore, the M21-1 manual dictates how adjudicators handle unsworn or 
uncertified testimony. If a statement is considered material to a favorable determination 
but lacks a certification under penalty of perjury, adjudicators are instructed to return a 
copy to the claimant for proper notarization or certification, rather than outright 
dismissing the evidence.

CAVC have systematically dismantled overly restrictive, anti-claimant VA practices, 
forcing the agency to respect the statutory weight of lay testimony.

Overcoming the Clinical Record Bias: Buchanan v. Nicholson 
Historically, VA adjudicators exhibited a strong, systemic bias against lay testimony that 
was not explicitly corroborated by military medical files. In Buchanan v. Nicholson, a 
veteran claimed service connection for schizophrenia, providing multiple lay affidavits 
from relatives, acquaintances, and a former unit sergeant detailing the severe onset of 
his psychiatric symptoms during his active duty service from 1973 to 1975. The Board of 
Veterans' Appeals (BVA) rejected the lay evidence entirely, stating that recollections of 
medical problems twenty years after service lacked credibility "absent confirmatory 
clinical records". 
The Federal Circuit firmly rejected this adjudicative practice, holding that the BVA 
applied a legally erroneous rule. The Court ruled that 38 U.S.C. § 1154(a) requires due 
consideration of lay evidence, and imposing a categorical requirement for 
contemporaneous medical corroboration renders the statute legally meaningless. 
Buchanan established the enduring precedent that while the VA may weigh the absence 
of medical records against lay evidence, it cannot dismiss a lay statement as incredible 
solely because official, contemporaneous medical documentation is missing. 
Expanding Lay Competence: Jandreau v. Nicholson and Barr v. 
Nicholson 
In Jandreau v. Nicholson, a veteran sought service connection for a dislocated right 
shoulder based on his own lay statements and a supportive buddy statement from a 
fellow serviceman. The BVA denied the claim, asserting that diagnosing a medical 
condition was entirely beyond the range of common lay knowledge and experience. 
The Federal Circuit reversed the decision, rejecting the VA's overly broad rule that 
medical evidence is universally required for any medical question. The Court articulated 
a tripartite framework defining when lay evidence is competent and sufficient to 
establish a diagnosis: 
1. When a layperson is competent to identify a relatively simple, observable 
condition (e.g., a broken bone or a dislocated shoulder). 
2. When the layperson is simply reporting a medical diagnosis that was 
communicated to them contemporaneously by a medical professional. 
3. When the layperson describes observable symptoms at the time of the event, 
which subsequently provide the factual foundation for a later diagnosis by a 
medical professional. 
Jandreau critically expanded the evidentiary power of lay statements, confirming that 
veterans do not need a medical degree to provide competent evidence regarding 
symptoms they personally experienced. This principle was further refined in Barr v. 
Nicholson, where the CAVC held that a layperson is entirely competent to identify 
conditions with "unique and readily identifiable features," such as varicose veins that are 

----- Page 9 -----
unnaturally distended or abnormally swollen, as determining their presence is an act of 
observation, not clinical deduction. 
Forcing Examiner Accountability: Miller v. Wilkie 
One of the most frequent administrative failures in the VA system occurs when a 
contracted C&P examiner issues a negative nexus opinion based entirely on a silent 
medical record, wholly ignoring the veteran's submitted lay statements detailing a 
history of continuous symptoms. 
In Miller v. Wilkie, the CAVC addressed this exact scenario. The Court held that a C&P 
examiner has a legal obligation to address relevant lay evidence when formulating a 
medical opinion. If an examiner formulates a negative opinion based solely on the 
absence of STR documentation while willfully ignoring the veteran's lay history of 
continuous symptoms, the examination fails to meet the standard of legal adequacy. 
Miller created a powerful enforcement mechanism, ensuring that lay statements are not 
just passively accepted into the record by adjudicators, but must be actively engaged 
with and analyzed by medical experts. If the examiner fails to do so, and the Board does 
not explicitly find the veteran's lay evidence incredible, the Court will presume the 
evidence is credible and order a new, adequate examination. 
Triggering the Duty to Assist: McLendon v. Nicholson 
Under the VCAA, the VA is required to provide a medical examination when the record 
indicates a disability may be associated with service. In McLendon v. Nicholson, the 
CAVC established that the evidentiary threshold for triggering this exam is exceptionally 
low. Medically competent evidence linking the current condition to service is not 
required to trigger an exam; credible lay evidence asserting an in-service injury and 
subsequent continuous symptoms is sufficient. This ensures that veterans who cannot 
afford private medical nexus opinions can still utilize lay statements to force the VA to 
fulfill its duty to assist and generate the necessary medical evidence at the 
government's expense. 
The Irrelevance of the Diagnostic Label: Clemons v. Shinseki 
Veterans frequently claim benefits for conditions using colloquial or incorrect medical 
terminology. In Clemons v. Shinseki, the CAVC ruled that when a veteran files a claim, 
they are ultimately seeking service connection for the symptoms they are experiencing, 
regardless of the specific medical label or diagnosis they place on the claim form. A 
veteran's lay description of their suffering—whether they call it "shell shock," "nerves," 
or "PTSD"—requires the VA to evaluate the actual symptomology presented in the lay 
evidence and apply the correct medical framework, rather than denying the claim simply 
because the veteran used the wrong diagnostic terminology. 
Procedural Mechanics and AMA Integration 
Understanding the legal theory of lay evidence must be paired with an understanding of 
the procedural mechanics required to inject this evidence into the VA system effectively, 
particularly under modern appellate frameworks. 
Standardized Forms and Unsworn Declarations 
While the VA is legally required to accept lay evidence written on a simple piece of 
paper, utilizing official forms ensures that the evidence is properly routed, scanned into 
the Veterans Benefits Management System (VBMS), and legally certified. 

----- Page 10 -----
• VA Form 21-10210 (Lay/Witness Statement): This is the primary form used for 
submitting third-party buddy statements from fellow service members, employers, 
spouses, or friends. It is explicitly designed to corroborate in-service events or 
detail the real-world impact of a condition. 
• VA Form 21-4138 (Statement in Support of Claim): Historically used for nearly 
all lay evidence, this form is typically utilized by the veteran to provide their own 
personal statement, detailing the onset of symptoms, treatment history, and daily 
functional limitations. 
Regardless of the format, strong lay statements generally utilize the language of 28 
U.S.C. § 1746, requiring a signature "under penalty of perjury". This formal certification 
establishes the basic threshold for presumed truthfulness, shifting the burden to the VA 
to find specific, articulable reasons to deem the statement incredible.

decisions. Under the AMA, veterans have three distinct review lanes following a rating

Appeals. The deployment of lay evidence is deeply intertwined with appropriate lane

Systemic Friction and Second-Order Implications 
Despite the robust statutory and jurisprudential framework protecting lay evidence, deep 
systemic friction remains within the VA's administrative execution, often resulting in 
improper denials that must be resolved on appeal. 
Office of Inspector General (OIG) Findings and Contract Examiner 
Failures 
Reports from the VA Office of Inspector General (OIG) have repeatedly highlighted 
severe deficiencies in how contract medical examiners process veteran claims. A June 
2022 OIG report titled "Contract Medical Exam Program Limitations Put Veterans at 
Risk" found that a significant percentage of C&P exams contained critical errors, 
including examiners failing to review the claims file or willfully ignoring the lay 
statements contained within it. The OIG estimated that errors were not corrected for 
approximately 35% of potentially insufficient exams before claims processors issued 
rating decisions based upon them. 
This systemic failure underscores a vital second-order implication of lay evidence: it 
serves as the ultimate protective mechanism for the veteran. Because contracted 
medical examiners are financially incentivized for volume rather than depth, they 
routinely default to denying causation when STRs are silent, taking the path of least 
resistance. If a veteran has not populated their file with highly specific, factual lay 
statements, an inadequate medical opinion goes unchallenged. However, when a file 
contains robust lay evidence, an examiner's failure to address it renders the exam 
defective under Miller v. Wilkie. This allows the veteran or their legal counsel to 
invalidate the negative medical opinion and force a remand, utilizing the procedural 
requirements of the law to counter the operational failures of the examination system. 
Furthermore, the OIG continually monitors the system for fraud, issuing alerts regarding 
fabricated Disability Benefits Questionnaires (DBQs) and pension poaching scams. 
While lay evidence is presumed credible, adjudicators are trained to look for 
inconsistencies between lay narratives and established medical facts, ensuring that the 
low threshold for accepting lay evidence is not exploited. 
The Friction Between Subjective Narratives and Objective Adjudication 
The VA's ongoing struggle with lay evidence represents a fundamental tension in 
administrative law: the attempt to quantify, standardize, and adjudicate subjective 
human suffering. Adjudicators (RVSRs) are tasked with processing claims highly 
efficiently, which inherently favors objective, easily scannable data such as MRI reports, 
audiograms, and definitive STR entries. Lay evidence, by contrast, introduces 
qualitative, narrative variability into a system designed for high-volume, quantitative 
output. 

----- Page 12 -----
When adjudicators improperly reject lay statements as "self-serving" or demand clinical 
corroboration where none is legally required, they violate the benefit of the doubt 
doctrine (38 U.S.C. § 5107) and the binding holdings of the Federal Circuit. The ongoing 
volume of remands from the CAVC and the Board of Veterans' Appeals demonstrates 
that lower-level claims processors continually struggle to properly weigh lay testimony in 
the absence of contemporaneous clinical records. The appellate courts act as a 
necessary counterweight, constantly reminding the agency that an absence of evidence 
in a military record is not substantive negative evidence against the veteran. 
Conclusion 
The legal framework governing witness lay statements in the Veterans Affairs disability 
claims process is highly developed, rooted in pro-claimant statutory mandates, and 
fiercely protected by appellate jurisprudence. Lay evidence is not merely 
supplementary; it is a formally recognized, legally competent form of proof capable of 
establishing historical facts, identifying observable medical conditions, corroborating 
combat and non-combat stressors, and demonstrating the true functional and 
occupational impairment of a disability. 
Through statutes like 38 U.S.C. § 1154 and doctrines established in seminal cases like 
Buchanan, Jandreau, Miller, and McLendon, the law expressly forbids the VA from 
discounting a veteran's lived experience simply because it lacks a contemporaneous 
medical stamp. In an administrative system overwhelmed by volume, historically 
hampered by missing records, and frequently hindered by inadequate contracted
