# Topic Checklist for Lay / Witness Statements

Use this checklist when REVIEWING an existing statement and when DRAFTING a new one. First
determine which topics are APPLICABLE to the statement's claim focus (e.g., PTSD / mental
health, need for regular assistance — Aid & Attendance / housebound / competency, TDIU, or a
physical condition). A topic that does not apply must be marked "not applicable" — never force
irrelevant topics onto a claim. For every applicable topic, a strong statement contains
concrete, observable, dated examples. There is NO one-page limit: be as detailed as necessary.

Core principle — translate symptoms into observable, dangerous consequences. Never settle for
"he has a bad memory"; describe the concrete risk it creates (e.g., almost double-dosed
life-sustaining medication, left the stove on).

## A. Hazards and Dangers (need for regular assistance)
Look for: fire hazards (stove left on, near-miss fires while cooking); medication
mismanagement (missed doses, accidental double-dosing); inability to keep the home secure
(doors left unlocked); an explicit point that the veteran needs a safe, supervised environment
and cannot safely be left alone.
Why it matters: directly targets the VA's regulatory criteria for mental incapacity requiring
regular assistance.

## B. Caregiver Burden and Necessity of Care
Look for: evidence that the care provided is not merely helpful but NECESSARY for the
veteran's physical safety and survival outside a clinical setting; who provides it (spouse /
family member), how constant it is, and what would likely happen without it.

## C. Basic Personal Care and Hygiene
Grooming/dressing: needs reminders or physical prompting to shower, brush teeth, or put on
clean clothes (e.g., during severe depressive episodes or from lack of motivation).
Feeding/nutrition: relies on the caregiver to prepare all meals, or does not eat regularly
without encouragement.

## D. Medication and Financial Management
Medication adherence: memory impairment or dissociation causing missed doses or dangerous
double-dosing; whether the caregiver must actively administer, monitor, or lock up medications
to ensure safety.
Managing finances: cognitive deficits, impulsivity, or memory problems that prevent safely
paying bills, managing bank accounts, or making sound financial decisions.

## E. Household Safety and Hazard Protection
Environmental dangers: memory loss, distraction, or dissociation creating hazards (stove left
on, doors not locked, near-miss fires while cooking).
Wandering/disorientation: episodes of severe hypervigilance or dissociation where the veteran
becomes disoriented in familiar surroundings or wanders away from safety.
Supervision for self-harm: if applicable, the need for constant vigilance to protect against
chronic suicidal ideation or degraded impulse control.

## F. Routine Errands and Chores
Community navigation: inability to grocery shop, attend school events, or run basic errands
due to panic attacks, intolerance of crowded spaces, or extreme anxiety.
Unsafe driving: the caregiver has taken over driving because of medication drowsiness, road
rage, or inability to focus on the road.
Household upkeep: physical or mental limitations preventing standard chores or shared family
responsibilities.

## G. Context and Symptom Progression
Before/after baseline: contrast demeanor, energy, and capabilities BEFORE the trauma/service
with how they changed AFTER returning.
Worsening timeline: how symptoms escalated over the years (e.g., occasional mild anxiety
evolving into frequent panic attacks, severe insomnia, emotional outbursts), with rough dates.

## H. Daily Life and Observable Behaviors
Factual observations: detailed, objective descriptions of distress — night sweats, pacing,
hypervigilance, night terrors, insomnia — WITHOUT psychiatric diagnosis. A medically trained
witness (e.g., Registered Nurse) can give especially precise observational detail but must
still stop short of diagnosing.
Loss of routine: daily tasks that have become overwhelming (crowded stores, household focus).

## I. Impact on Family Dynamics
Marriage/connection: emotional withdrawal, irritability, difficulty maintaining communication
and intimacy.
Parenting/socializing: unpredictable moods or isolation affecting interactions with children;
concrete examples of missed family gatherings and social events.

## J. Physical Side Effects and Secondary Conditions from Medications
Timeline of medication-triggered changes: significant weight gain after psychotropic
medications followed by sleep-apnea-type symptoms (witness observes gasping for air at night);
observable hypertension or gastrointestinal symptoms whose onset coincided with starting the
medications.

## K. Functional Impairments from Medication Side Effects
Drowsiness / diminished reaction times → driving duties taken over by the caregiver.
Cognitive fog (worsened memory loss, dissociation, confusion) → necessitates supervision to
protect the veteran from daily household hazards.

## L. Formatting and Certification (required in every statement)
Clear identification: writer's full name, contact information, and explicit relationship to the
veteran. Truthfulness: a signed and dated closing certification affirming the contents are true
and accurate to the best of the writer's knowledge. Detail is expected — no page limit.

## Writer guidelines
- Describe observable facts and behaviors only — strictly no medical diagnoses or clinical
  jargon; reword any diagnosis as observation or attributed speech ("his doctor told him...").
- Provide as many specific, real-world examples as necessary; formal correspondence drafts are
  not constrained to one page.
- Statements from spouses, family, and friends are the most valuable evidence of how the
  veteran's life changed since the trauma: continuity of symptoms and functional effects on
  daily life, emotional well-being, and employability; before/after behavioral comparisons;
  family-life impacts (emotional distance, skipped gatherings, unpredictable anger); and severe
  symptoms (frequency of night terrors, insomnia, panic attacks, daily-environment hazards).
