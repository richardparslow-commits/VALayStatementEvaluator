# Legal Framework for VA Lay / Witness Statements

Use this framework when evaluating or drafting lay statements. Cite the relevant authority
when it materially supports a finding.

## Governing statutes and regulations

- **38 U.S.C. § 1154(a)** — VA must give due consideration to all pertinent medical AND lay
  evidence, along with the places, types, and circumstances of the veteran's service.
- **38 U.S.C. § 5107(b) — Benefit of the Doubt** — when the positive and negative evidence is
  in approximate balance (equipoise), the benefit of the doubt goes to the claimant. The veteran
  need only bring evidence to a 50/50 balance, not prove the claim beyond a reasonable doubt.
- **38 C.F.R. § 3.159(a)(2)** — competent lay evidence means testimony by a person with
  knowledge of the facts; it is competent for matters the person observed and is relevant to.
- **38 C.F.R. § 3.303(a)** — service connection generally requires evidence of incurrence or
  aggravation in service plus a current disability.
- **38 C.F.R. § 3.303(b)** — continuity of symptomatology for chronic conditions: lay evidence
  of continuous symptoms since service can support the claim even absent continuous treatment.
- **Duty to assist (38 U.S.C. § 5103A)** — VA has an affirmative duty to help develop evidence;
  detailed lay evidence can trigger the duty to provide a VA examination.

## The Caluza Triangle (Caluza v. Brown, 7 Vet. App. 498 (1995))

Service connection requires three elements; lay statements can support each, within limits:

1. **Current diagnosis** — usually medical evidence; lay evidence may identify observable
   conditions (see Jandreau).
2. **In-service event/incurrence/aggravation** — lay evidence is often the PRIMARY evidence
   here, especially for undocumented events, stressors, and injuries.
3. **Nexus (causal link)** — generally requires a medical opinion; lay evidence can support
   nexus for simple observable conditions, or continuity of symptoms under 3.303(b).

## Controlling case law on lay evidence

- **Jandreau v. Nicholson, 492 F.3d 1372 (Fed. Cir. 2007)** — lay evidence is competent to
  diagnose or identify conditions that are simple and readily observable by a layperson.
- **Buchanan v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006)** — lay evidence may establish an
  in-service event and nexus via continuous symptoms; absence of contemporaneous medical
  records is NOT, standing alone, a valid reason to reject lay evidence.
- **Davidson v. Shinseki, 581 F.3d 1313 (Fed. Cir. 2009)** — lay testimony can establish
  nexus; a medical opinion is not always required.
- **Barr v. Nicholson, 21 Vet. App. 303 (2007)** — lay competence depends on the disability
  claimed: a layperson may identify a condition that is simple and readily observable by its
  features (there, varicose veins that are "unnaturally distended or abnormally swollen and
  tortuous"), because doing so is not "medical in nature". Competence is not a blanket bar.
- **Washington v. Nicholson, 19 Vet. App. 362 (2005)** — a veteran is competent to report what
  he experienced; lay evidence about observable symptoms is competent.
- **Caluza v. Brown** — credibility factors: facial plausibility, consistency, personal
  interest, demeanor; the Board must analyze credibility and give reasons.
- **Miller v. Wilkie** — a medical exam/opinion that fails to address the veteran's detailed
  lay history is inadequate.
- **McLendon v. Nicholson, 20 Vet. App. 79 (2006)** — low threshold to trigger VA's duty to
  provide an examination; credible lay evidence of continuity can meet it.
- **Stegall v. West, 11 Vet. App. 268 (1998)** — VA must comply with remand orders, including
  obtaining compliant exams that address the lay record.
- **Buczynski v. Shinseki, 24 Vet. App. 221, 224 (2011)** — the Board may not treat silence in
  the record as negative evidence unless it is the sort of condition or symptom that would
  normally be noted or reported.
- **Horn v. Shinseki, 25 Vet. App. 231, 239 n.7 (2012)** — the absence of evidence cannot be
  treated as substantive negative evidence without a foundation showing that the missing fact
  is the kind of fact that would ordinarily have been recorded. A gap proves something only
  where the record would have been made.
- **Mitchell v. Shinseki** — where pain causes functional loss, the disability is rated as if
  the same loss had another cause; pain-mediated loss is not a lesser kind of loss.

## Absence of evidence vs. negative evidence

These are different findings, and only one of them is evidence against a claim:

- **Absence of evidence** — the record is silent, omits the topic, or fails to mention a fact.
  This is not substantive negative evidence against the claimant (Buczynski v. Shinseki,
  24 Vet. App. 221, 224 (2011); Horn v. Shinseki, 25 Vet. App. 231, 239 n.7 (2012); M21-1,
  Part V, Subpart ii, Ch. 1, § A). Most lay evidence of symptoms, function, and home life
  falls here by nature: nobody writes those things down.
- **Negative evidence** — a record entry that *positively* states the contrary: a dated
  normal finding, "denies pain", "no tenderness", "gait normal", the other side, an
  incompatible date or facility. Only this conflicts with a claim.
- **When a gap is meaningful** — only for facts that would ordinarily be documented (surgery,
  hospitalization, a fracture, imaging, a prescription) — silence counts as negative evidence
  only where the condition or symptom is the sort that would normally be noted or reported
  (Buczynski). Even then the correct finding is that the record set does not address the
  claim, and the remedy is to obtain the missing document (duty to assist, 38 U.S.C.
  § 5103A) — not to treat the gap as a refutation.
- **Several factors cut against holding a gap against the veteran**: the absence of
  contemporaneous records is not, standing alone, a reason to reject lay evidence (Buchanan
  v. Nicholson, 451 F.3d 1331 (Fed. Cir. 2006)); the evidentiary balance need only reach
  equipoise (38 U.S.C. § 5107(b)); and for combat veterans, satisfactory lay or other evidence
  of incurrence in service is to be accepted as sufficient proof absent clear and convincing
  evidence to the contrary (38 U.S.C. § 1154(b)).
- **Consequence for an examiner or the Board**: an examination or opinion that never
  addresses the veteran's detailed lay history is inadequate for the matter it decides
  (Barr for competence; Miller v. Wilkie; Stegall v. West).

## Musculoskeletal functional loss (38 C.F.R. §§ 4.40, 4.45, 4.59)

A joint or spine rating is not a measurement contest — it turns on **functional loss**:

- **38 C.F.R. § 4.40** — the rating covers loss of function of the joint or muscle group:
  limitation of motion, and other functional loss such as pain, weakness, fatigability, and
  incoordination.
- **38 C.F.R. § 4.45(f)** — the factors to be looked for: less movement than normal, weakened
  movement, excess fatigability, incoordination, pain on movement, disturbance of locomotion,
  disturbance in weight-bearing, swelling, deformity, atrophy.
- **38 C.F.R. § 4.59** — with any form of arthritis or musculoskeletal disease, painful motion
  is an important factor of disability, and actually painful, unstable, or malaligned joints
  are entitled to at least the minimum compensable evaluation; "painful motion" is treated as
  limiting motion.
- **DeLuca v. Brown, 8 Vet. App. 202 (1995)** — in rating musculoskeletal disability, VA must
  consider functional loss due to pain, weakness, fatigability, incoordination, and flare-ups
  under §§ 4.40, 4.45 and 4.59; a rating based on the schedular minimum does not dispose of
  that consideration.
- **Sharp v. Shulkin, 29 Vet. App. 26 (2017)** — where flare-ups are raised by the evidence,
  they must be addressed, including whether they affect functional loss.
- **What this means for a lay statement**: the witness supplies the *functional* facts — pain
  on movement, reduced distance/walking time before stopping, what a flare costs him and how
  often it happens, weakness and giving way, disturbed sleep, who now does the task. A lay
  witness does not supply range-of-motion values or percentage ratings, and the absence of a
  measured number is not the absence of disability. Where the record examination measured the
  joint only at rest and never addressed painful motion, functional loss, or flare-ups, that
  examination is inadequate for rating purposes and an opinion addressing those factors should
  be requested.

## Lay competence boundaries (what a layperson MAY and MAY NOT assert)

MAY (competent lay evidence):
- Firsthand sensory observations: what the witness saw, heard, felt, smelled.
- Observable symptoms (limping, grimacing, nightmares, panic, trembling, isolation).
- The onset of symptoms and their continuity over time, in sequence ("it started when … and
  it has hurt ever since"), without naming a cause.
- What the veteran can and cannot do: distance walked before stopping, stairs, lifting,
  dressing, driving, and how these have changed (the functional equivalents of lost motion).
  They describe the function; they do not state range-of-motion values or ratings.
- Dates, places, and descriptions of events the witness personally witnessed.
- Changes over time in behavior, mood, function, appearance.
- Impact on work, family, and social life the witness directly observed.
- Identification of readily observable conditions (visible varicose veins, skin conditions,
  obvious gait abnormalities) per Jandreau.

MAY NOT (outside lay competence — must come from a medical professional):
- Formal medical diagnoses of non-obvious conditions.
- Medical etiology/causation opinions ("his back pain was caused by the 1998 lift").
- Interpretation of imaging, labs, or test results.
- Prognoses, rating-percentage assertions, or measured range-of-motion values ("I am 40%
  disabled"; "the knee bends to 90 degrees"), and legal conclusions ("he meets the criteria
  for TDIU"). The functional consequence is lay testimony; the percentage is not.

A statement that strays into medical opinion is weaker, not stronger; flag it and recommend
rewording as observation ("I saw him unable to stand for more than a few minutes").

## Forms and submission

- **VA Form 21-10210 (Lay/Witness Statement)** — preferred form since Jan 2021 (current edition
  Jul 2024). Sections: I veteran identification; II claimant identification (if not the
  veteran); III the statement itself ("Describe what you yourself know or have observed about
  the facts or circumstances relevant to this claim"); IV witness contact info and relationship;
  V certification ("I CERTIFY that I have completed this statement and that its information is
  true and correct to the best of my knowledge and belief") + signature + date.
- One form per witness. Addenda allowed if Section III runs out of space (note on the form).
- VA Form 21-4138 (Statement in Support of Claim) may still be used, especially for the
  veteran's own statements.
- Unsworn declarations are acceptable under 28 U.S.C. § 1746 language ("I declare under penalty
  of perjury under the laws of the United States of America that the foregoing is true and
  correct") when the witness's full name, address, and date are included.
- Penalty warning: willfully false statements carry fines/imprisonment — accuracy and honesty
  are not optional; they are legally required.

## Weighing factors adjudicators apply (and that the app should check)

Credibility and probative value depend on: facial plausibility; internal consistency;
consistency with the record (medical and service records); personal interest/bias; level of
detail and specificity; the witness's opportunity to observe (how often, over what period, in
what settings); and temporal proximity of observations to the events described.
